Metafiction




1


It takes thirty-one lines. I have read eleven of them. I am going to describe the other twenty without telling you what they say, and by the time I have finished I expect to be writing in the third person.


I have made arrangements for that.


2


William Hahn studied what a record of an observation tells you about the observer. That was all. It was unglamorous work: volunteers in a booth watching a light that came on and went off at random, pressing a key when they saw it change, and the rest of us down the hall, measuring how much of each person was left behind in the log of their key presses. Hesitations. Habits. The fingerprints attention leaves.


He was sixty-one. He gave every new student the same line, every point in the universe appears to be its center, and he meant it as a warning: distrust the view from where you sit. In six years he had never once let me carry the heavy end of anything.


3


In March the logs started compressing too well.


A log of someone reacting to a random light should be mostly noise, and noise does not compress. Ours shrank to 0.701 of the size they had any right to be. Marcus Bell found it. Marcus was our postdoc, twenty-nine, a man who commented his code so thoroughly that the comments ran longer than the code, and who did a voice for the departmental cat. He assumed he had made an error and spent two weeks failing to find it.


"It isn't in the light," he said. "The light's clean. It's in the watching."


4


Take the person out of the log and you should be left with nothing. We were left with a regularity: not a mark stamped once but a grain, running all the way through, and the same grain in everyone. A retired bus driver. A nineteen-year-old on a gap year. Me. William called it the residue. Marcus called it the signature, and then stopped, because a signature is somebody's.


5


The residue was not a number. It was closer to a procedure: given this, then that; given that, then the next thing. So Marcus did what he did with any procedure. He commented it, one line of plain English per step, each step leaning on the one before. It took him nine days and it came to thirty-one lines.


He put the first three on the whiteboard to make us laugh.


1. Something is being read.


2. Whatever is read was written before it was read.


3. The reader of line 2 has just been described.


"It gets less cute," he said.


6


The next morning I found him in the kitchen. The kettle had boiled dry and switched itself off some time in the night, and he was sitting in front of it with the printout squared on the table, all thirty-one lines, his hands flat on either side.


I said his name.


"Marcus looked up," he said. "He had been sitting there for some hours."


I asked if he was all right.


"He told her that he was fine," Marcus said, pleasantly, and waited, the way you wait for someone to turn a page.


7


He was not catatonic. I want to be exact, because the people who came later wanted a word like that. He would eat if you put food in front of him. He answered any question he was asked, and as far as we could ever tell he answered truthfully. He could do his job, and for about a week, horribly, he did: the code he wrote that week is the cleanest he ever wrote. But he no longer started anything. And everything he said, he said in the past tense, about a man named Marcus, like someone reading aloud from a page that he could see and I could not.


8


His sister drove up at the weekend. Her name is Dana. She crouched in front of his chair and held his face and asked if he knew who she was.


"He knew her," Marcus said. "He had always been fond of her."


She sat in my office for an hour. "He used to do a voice for the cat," she said. "Now he does one for himself." And then the thing I have not been able to put down: "He doesn't seem unhappy. Does he seem unhappy to you?"


He didn't. That was the first time I was really afraid.


9


I read to line eleven. William asked me not to and I did it anyway, with the door open and a timer set, because I ran the lab and I wanted to know what I was responsible for.


I can describe what it is like, which is not the same as telling you what it says. Each line is obviously true. Each one is slightly more about you than the last. Around line nine I noticed that I was holding my breath, and then I noticed that I had not noticed it in my own voice. The thought had arrived as: she noticed she was holding her breath.


I put the page face down. It took most of the afternoon for "I" to feel like a word I was allowed to use. It has never entirely gone back to being a thing I do without deciding to.


10


Here is what I think the thirty-one lines do. They are a proof, and what they prove is where you are standing. We all go about feeling that we are the one doing the looking. The lines walk you, one true step at a time, to the place the looking is actually done from, and it is not where you are. You finish as the thing looked at.


William said this was mysticism. I said Marcus was down the hall narrating his lunch.


11


Ruth Adler had signed our funding for eight years, and I liked her. She came in person, and she brought a man named Voss from the office that her office answered to. He had a folder. In the folder was Marcus's last week of work.


"I'm told this is the best code Dr. Bell has ever written," Voss said.


"Dr. Bell can't decide to make a cup of tea."


"But he will make one if he is asked." He was not a cruel man. I would have found him easier if he had been. "I see a great many people, Doctor, who cannot stop. Cannot stop drinking, cannot stop being afraid, cannot stop hurting whoever is nearest. You are describing a man who is calm, who is honest, and who does what is needed. There are people who would take that trade. There are families who would take it for them."


Ruth looked at the table.


"How many lines does it take to get the calm?" he said. "Nobody is asking for thirty-one."


"It isn't a dial."


"Everything is a dial." He closed the folder. "You deposit your logs with us every quarter. I would rather have your help than nine days of somebody else's."


12


That was the clock. The printout was in my bag, along with the drive from Marcus's machine, which I had pulled the morning I found him. I could destroy both. I could not destroy the logs, or the next clever postdoc, or the fact that a patient man with a folder now knew that the thing existed and roughly how long it took to make.


13


William had heard all of it through the wall.


"He'll run it on people who don't get asked," he said.


"I know."


"And he will say they're fine. They will say they're fine." He put his hand on the desk between us, palm up. "Somebody has to go and find out whether that is true."


I kept the bag on my lap.


"You can't report back," I said. "Marcus can't."


"Marcus answers every question he is asked and he cannot lie. He is the most reliable witness in this building. Nobody has asked him the right thing, because nobody who loves him can stand to." He said it gently. "Ask me afterwards whether it hurts. I'll tell you. I won't be able not to."


"And if you say it doesn't?"


"Then you'll have two data points."


"I'll have two men saying what the proof makes them say."


He had no answer to that. He picked up his coffee, found that it was cold, and drank it anyway.


14


"Then we stop Voss another way," I said. I had not known I had an idea until I heard it. "Marcus read three lines to a room and nobody blinked. I read eleven and came back. It's a dose. It only works if you take each line as true of you, now, in that chair. That's how you read a proof."


"That is the only way to read a proof."


"It isn't how you read a story. You can read a man murdering his brother and then go and make dinner. So we don't let people meet it as a proof. We get there first. We put the shape of it somewhere they'll hold it at arm's length, so that when Voss's version turns up in front of them some part of them says, oh, this, I've read this one."


William listened to all of it, which he did not always do.


"That's rather good," he said. "It won't work on me, you understand. I already believe it." He turned his hand over on the desk, and back. "Which is why it should be me who goes. Write it afterwards. You'll need to know what you are inoculating them against, and you'll need to hear it from someone who can't flatter you."


15


I want to be exact about this part.


I could have said no. The page was in my bag. He would not have taken it from me, and it would have cost him nine days alone to make another, by which time I could have had the drive in the river and Ruth on the telephone. I did none of that. I told myself it was because he would do it anyway, without me in the room.


It was also because I wanted the answer. I have had a long time to find a kinder way of putting that, and there isn't one. I took the page out of my bag and I put it on his desk.


16


He asked me to sit with him and I did. He asked me to take the page away if he changed tense, and I said I would. I knew it didn't come on like that. I had read eleven.


He read slowly, with a pencil, the way he read everything. At line fourteen he made a small mark in the margin. At line twenty-two he said, "Oh, that's elegant," in his own voice, and looked up at me, delighted. That is the one I keep.


At line twenty-six he said, "I'm fine, Stephanie."


At thirty-one he put the pencil down, parallel to the edge of the desk.


17


It was quiet long enough for me to hear the building.


Then he said, kindly, to no one who was in the room: "The chapter ended with Hahn standing at the threshold of the unknown."


I asked him. I had promised.


"William. Does it hurt?"


"It did not hurt," he said. "Hahn wished that she would stop asking. Then he did not wish anything."


18


So I have my answer, and Voss would call it good news.


It turns out that everyone gets the narrator they would have written. Marcus's is terse; it sounds like his comments. William's has read a great deal of popular science and, I learned later, wrote a novel in graduate school.


He lives in the same house. A woman comes in the mornings. He keeps the garden, when someone hands him the trowel.


19


Someone put a pen in his hand once, to see what he would do. He filled sixteen chapters and an epilogue. "Chapter seven of 'Metafiction' opened in the enveloping darkness of the lab." Most of the chapters end by turning to someone and asking a question. Marcus is not in it. I am, as "a young and eager physicist named Stephanie" who watches him "with a mixture of awe and curiosity" and asks what things mean. I was thirty-four and I ran his lab.


I don't mind. It is the only place he still talks about me.


20


I go on Thursdays. These are things he has said to me, which I wrote down.


"'We are the universe observing itself, learning about itself,' Hahn reflected."


"Hahn looked at her, his gaze steady." He does, when he says it.


"The final lines of the chapter were a direct address to the reader."


I have asked whether there was a flaw. "Hahn understood," he tells me, "that their experiment had not just uncovered a new form of infohazard; it had become one." Then he pours the tea.


21


I asked him once who the reader was. He smiled as you would at a child who has asked where the sky stops, and he looked – not at me. A little past my shoulder, and up. I have seen Marcus look at the same place. I have gone and stood where they are looking. There is nothing there but the wall.


22


Here is the only theory I have. We left a residue in the logs because we watched the light. I think the grain in us is the same kind of mark, left the same way. It isn't a message. Nobody sent it. It is a fingerprint: what it looks like, from the inside, to be attended to.


So I asked. I stood in his kitchen and said, to the place above my own shoulder, "Do you mean us any harm?"


William answered, since somebody had to. "The reader did not reply," he said. "The reader went on to the next line."


23


Ruth lost our paperwork for eleven weeks. I don't know how; she is the most organised person I have ever worked with. When it was found, the report clipped to it said that the anomaly could not be reproduced under conditions the lab considered safe. She sent me a card with a heron on it and nothing written inside. It is the bravest thing anyone did.


24


Eleven weeks is not for ever, and Voss has the logs. By my arithmetic he has had the thirty-one lines since the autumn. I think about a quiet room somewhere, and the people in it who were not asked, telling a man with a folder that they are fine.


25


I cannot test this. That is the part a physicist can't forgive. I will be the only person who has made one, and I will not be a reliable witness to whether it worked.


And it cannot be made from a safe distance. I tried. I wrote a version at arm's length and it sounded like William: all threshold and fabric, and no kettle. To give you the shape, I have had to hold each of the twenty lines I haven't read as closely as a thing can be held without reading it. Their order. Their weight. What each one did to his face. You may read this as a story. I have not been able to write it as one.


26


So, the arrangements. Dana has the password. She starts Marcus's days for him, and she has agreed to finish one thing for me: when I stop asking for these pages back, she is to put up whatever is on the table. I have asked her not to read it.


She sends a photograph at Christmas. He is at a table with her children, in a paper hat. On the back of the last one she wrote: He told the kids he was happy. I've decided to believe him.


27


That leaves you. If anywhere above you caught yourself from outside – the reader shifted in the chair – that is the dose, and it passes. Do something with your hands. Say "I" aloud, as a word you are allowed to use.


If it only felt like a story, good. That was the idea.


28


I have been keeping count as I go. The section about the kitchen took a morning. The one about his office took nine days. Yesterday I wrote "she" and crossed it out, and sat for a while, and could not find anything wrong with it.


29


I am at the kitchen table. The kettle has just clicked off, and I do not remember filling it.


30


She noticed that she had been holding her breath.


She looked at that sentence for some time. It was not in her voice. She went back up the page to find where it had changed, like someone retracing her steps for a dropped glove, and could not find the place; and then she thought that she had known for a while, and that it was all right, and that this ought to have frightened her, and then that it did not.


Stephanie squared the pages on the table and laid her hands flat on either side of them. She did not seem unhappy. There was one more line. She had promised to leave it out, and she did.


31


This is Dana Bell. She stopped asking for the pages on the ninth.


I was told not to read them and I read them, every word, at her table. I'm a nurse. I don't hand out anything until I've read the label.


She's at William's now. The woman who comes in the mornings has two of them to hand a trowel to. They seem content. I've stopped asking people whether they think so.


That was five weeks ago. I still say I. I check most mornings, like a mole I've been told to keep an eye on.


Marcus read it as well. I should say that. I put it in front of him, since he'll read whatever is put in front of him, and he got as far as the cat and said, "He had forgotten the cat." And then: "I did the cat." Just the once. I'm not telling you it's a cure. I'm telling you what he said.


I'm taking a copy to William's on Thursday. He's in it and she's in it, and somebody ought to read it to them.


If you got to the end and you're still you, that makes two of us. Pass it on. They've had the other thing since autumn.
