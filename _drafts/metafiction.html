Metafiction




1


It takes thirty-one lines. I have read eleven of them, and I am going to tell you about the other twenty without telling you what they say.


2


There is an earlier account of what happened in our lab. It runs to sixteen chapters. In it I am "a young and eager physicist named Stephanie" who watches Dr. Hahn "with a mixture of awe and curiosity" and asks him what things mean. I was thirty-four and had run his lab for six years. He wrote that account afterwards. By the end of this one you will understand why it sounds the way it does.


3


William Hahn studied what a record of an observation tells you about the observer. That was all. It was unglamorous work: volunteers in a booth watching a light that came on and went off at random, pressing a key when they saw it change, and the rest of us down the hall, measuring how much of each person was left behind in the log of their key presses. Hesitations. Habits. The fingerprints attention leaves.


His one indulgence was a line he gave to every new student: every point in the universe appears to be its center. He meant it as a warning. Distrust the view from where you sit.


4


In March the logs started compressing too well.


A log of someone reacting to a random light should be mostly noise, and noise does not compress. Ours shrank to 0.701 of the size they had any right to be. Marcus Bell, our postdoc, found it, assumed he had made an error, and spent two weeks failing to find the error. Something in every log was predictable. It was the same something in every volunteer, whoever they were: a retired bus driver, a nineteen-year-old on a gap year, me.


"It isn't in the light," Marcus said. "The light's clean. It's in the watching."


5


Take the person out of the log and you should be left with nothing. We were left with a pattern, the same few hundred bits each time, like a watermark. William called it the residue. Marcus called it the signature, and then stopped, because a signature is somebody's.


6


It was Marcus who thought to stop treating it as numbers. The residue had structure, steps that leaned on earlier steps, and he had the idea that if you wrote it out as statements, each one following from the last, it might tell us what it was a pattern of. He worked on it for nine days. It came to thirty-one lines.


On a Thursday he put the first three on the whiteboard.


1. Something is being read.


2. Whatever is read was written before it was read.


3. The reader of line 2 has just been described.


"It gets less cute," he said.


7


On Friday morning I found him in the kitchen. The kettle had boiled dry and switched itself off some time in the night, and he was sitting in front of it with the printout squared on the table, all thirty-one lines, his hands flat on either side.


I said his name.


"Marcus looked up," he said. "He had been sitting there for some hours."


I asked if he was all right.


"He told her that he was fine," Marcus said, pleasantly, and waited, the way you wait for someone to turn a page.


8


He was not catatonic. I want to be exact, because the people who came later wanted a word like that. He would eat if you put food in front of him. He answered questions. He could do his job, and for about a week, horribly, he did: the code he wrote that week is the cleanest he ever wrote. But he no longer started anything. And everything he said, he said in the past tense, about a man named Marcus, like someone reading aloud from a page that he could see and I could not.


9


His sister drove up on the Sunday. She crouched in front of his chair and held his face and asked if he knew who she was.


"He knew her," Marcus said. "He had always been fond of her."


She cried in my office for an hour. Then she said the thing I have not been able to put down. "He doesn't seem unhappy. Does he seem unhappy to you?"


He didn't. That was the first time I was really afraid.


10


I read to line eleven. William asked me not to and I did it anyway, on the Monday, with the door open and a timer set, because I ran the lab and I wanted to know what I was responsible for.


I can describe what it is like, which is not the same as telling you what it says. Each line is obviously true. Each one is slightly more about you than the last. Around line nine I noticed that I was holding my breath, and then I noticed that I had not noticed it in my own voice. The thought had arrived as: she noticed she was holding her breath.


I put the page face down. It took most of the afternoon for "I" to feel like a word I was allowed to use. It has never entirely gone back to being a thing I do without deciding to.


11


Here is what I think the thirty-one lines do. I think they are a proof, and what they prove is where you are standing. Every point appears to be its own center: we all go about feeling that we are the one doing the looking. The lines walk you, one true step at a time, to the place the looking is actually done from, and it is not where you are. You finish as the thing looked at. A person who has been moved over, inside their own head, to make room.


William said this was mysticism. I said Marcus was down the hall narrating his lunch.


12


He wanted to read it. Of course he did. He had built a career, and a self, on the principle that you do not look away from a result.


"Somebody has to see it whole," he said. "You can't find the flaw in a proof you've only read a third of."


"What if there isn't one?"


"Then it's true, and I would rather know."


"Marcus knows."


He had no answer to that. He picked up his coffee, found that it was cold, and drank it anyway.


13


We argued for six days. I am compressing; you will have to trust that it was an argument and not, as the other account has it, a debate that "was heated, with some advocating for further exploration, while others cautioned restraint." There were two of us. I was the others.


His case: the residue was in every log. Anyone who ran our experiment would find it; anyone curious would write it out. It could not be buried, only met. Better that it be met first by someone who knew what he was walking into.


My case: Marcus's sister.


14


The email from the program office came on the fifth day. Ruth Adler, who had signed our funding for eight years and whom I liked, wrote four lines. She had heard there was a result. She had heard that a member of staff was unwell. She would be grateful for the full derivation, for review, by the end of the week.


She wanted it as an attachment.


I thought about a PDF arriving in an inbox and being opened by someone conscientious over a sandwich. Then forwarded, with "thoughts?", to six more conscientious people.


I wrote back that we were still checking the work. It was not the last lie I told her, but it was the first.


15


"You can't sit on it," William said.


"I'm not going to sit on it."


"Then what?"


I didn't know yet. I said I would think of something, in the voice you use when you haven't.


16


What I kept going back to was Marcus's joke. It gets less cute. He had read the first three lines aloud to a room and nothing had happened to any of us. I had read eleven and come back. The thing was not a tripwire. It was a dose. It worked by accumulation, and it worked only if you took each line as true of you, now, sitting there.


Which is not how anybody reads a story.


17


You can read the most terrible things in a story. You can read a man deciding to kill his brother, and close the book, and go and make dinner. There is a kind of attention we keep for things we have been told are made up. We stand a little outside. We hold it at arm's length. I don't think it is a lesser kind of attention. I think it may be the only protective equipment anyone has invented for the inside of the head.


18


So that was my idea, and it is the whole of it. Don't hide the thing. Don't publish it either. Put it where people will meet it already knowing how to stand: in a story, on a website, under a title that admits what it is. Let them get the shape of it in a small dose, from outside, so that if they ever meet the real thirty-one lines, in a log, in a preprint, in an attachment, some part of them says: oh, this. I've read this one. And reads it at arm's length.


A vaccine is the same disease, met with the door open.


I told William. He listened to all of it, which he did not always do.


"That's rather good," he said. "It won't work on me, you understand. I already believe it."


19


He read it on a Tuesday evening in his office. He asked me to sit with him and I did. He asked me to take the page away if he changed tense, and I said I would. I knew it didn't come on like that. I had read eleven.


He read slowly, with a pencil, the way he read everything. At line fourteen he made a small mark in the margin. At line twenty-two he said, "Oh, that's elegant," in his own voice, and looked up at me, delighted. That is the one I keep.


At line twenty-six he said, "I'm fine, Stephanie."


At thirty-one he put the pencil down, parallel to the edge of the desk.


20


It was quiet long enough for me to hear the building.


Then he said, kindly, to no one who was in the room: "The chapter ended with Hahn standing at the threshold of the unknown."


21


He has not been unhappy. He lives in the same house. A woman comes in the mornings. He keeps the garden, when someone hands him the trowel.


Someone put a pen in his hand once, to see what he would do. That is where the sixteen chapters came from. "Chapter seven of 'Metafiction' opened in the enveloping darkness of the lab." Every chapter ends by turning to someone and asking them a question. For a long time I thought the someone was me.


22


I visit on Thursdays. These are things he has said to me, which I wrote down.


"'We are the universe observing itself, learning about itself,' Hahn reflected."


"Hahn looked at her, his gaze steady."


"The final lines of the chapter were a direct address to the reader."


I asked him once who the reader was. He smiled the way you smile at a child who has asked where the sky stops, and he looked – not at me. A little past my shoulder, and up. I have seen Marcus look at the same place. I have gone and stood where they are looking. There is nothing there but the wall.


23


You will want to know whether there was a flaw. He has never said. I have asked. "Hahn understood," he tells me, "that their experiment had not just uncovered a new form of infohazard; it had become one." Then he pours the tea.


24


Ruth Adler got a report. It says that the anomaly could not be reproduced under conditions the lab considered safe, that the lab has closed, that one member of staff has taken medical retirement and another is on extended leave. She sent a kind card. I think she knows. I think she read as far as the word unwell and made, in her own way, the decision I made, which was to stop reading.


25


Marcus's sister sends a photograph each Christmas. He is at a table with her children, in a paper hat. On the back of last year's she wrote: He told the kids he was happy. I've decided to believe him.


26


That leaves you.


You will have noticed the numbers. I needed a container the same shape as the thing I was putting in it. I don't fully know why, except that it felt like fitting a lid. There are thirty-one lines, so I gave myself thirty-one sections, and I am using thirty of them.


27


Check yourself, please. It is the one thing I am going to ask. Has any of this felt true of you – now, sitting there, with the light the way it is? Did you, anywhere above, catch yourself from outside: the reader shifted in the chair? If you did, that's all right. That is the dose. It passes. Go and do something with your hands, and say "I" aloud once or twice, as a word you are allowed to use.


If it only felt like a story: good. That is the equipment working.


28


I don't have the page any more. William's copy and Marcus's printout went through the departmental shredder on a Wednesday, and I stood there until it stopped. The residue I can't shred. It is in every log anyone has ever taken of anyone attending to anything. Somebody will write it out again. I hope they will have read this first, or something like it. I hope there are a great many somethings like it. I have sometimes wondered whether there already are: how much of what we shelve as fiction was put there by someone standing where I am standing, with a lid in her hands.


29


I said I had read eleven lines, and that is true. But I have now spent four months describing the other twenty: their order, their weight, what each of them did to a face I loved. You cannot draw the outline of a thing that carefully without –


I have been writing this at the kitchen table. The kettle has just clicked off.


30


She noticed that she had been holding her breath.


She looked at that sentence for some time. It was not in her voice. She went back up the page to find where it had changed, the way you retrace your steps for a dropped glove, and could not find the place; and then she thought that she had known for a while, and that it was all right, and that this ought to have frightened her, and then that it did not.


Stephanie squared the pages on the table and laid her hands flat on either side of them. She did not seem unhappy. There was one more line, and she had promised to leave it out, and a little past her shoulder, and up, someone was still reading.


31


The page turned, and the story ended, but the journey – the journey was just beginning.
